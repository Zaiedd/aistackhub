It's 10:15 PM two nights before the midterm. You have three lecture PDFs, a 40-page chapter, and notes that look like a crime scene. The old way was to re-read everything and hope something sticks. The 2026 way is different — if you pick the right tools and stop treating AI like a magic answer machine.

I ran the same set of course materials through the main study tools students actually use right now and timed what got me from raw files to something I could actively recall under pressure. The difference between a useful stack and a waste of time is not which model is "smarter." It is whether the tool forces you to engage with *your* sources and whether it turns that material into practice, not just another summary.

## The real jobs the week before an exam

Most students need three things in the final stretch:

1. **Turn your own materials into something reviewable** without re-typing everything.
2. **Force active recall** instead of passive re-reading.
3. **Get unstuck on a specific concept** without being handed the full answer.

Everything else is optional. That filter eliminates a lot of shiny tools.

## Start with source-grounded study — NotebookLM (Gemini Notebook)

Google's NotebookLM (now often labeled Gemini Notebook) is still the strongest free starting point for most university courses. Upload the actual PDFs, slides, and notes that belong to the class. Every answer is forced to cite a passage from those sources. As of September 2026 the free tier still allows 100 notebooks and 50 sources per notebook — enough for one or two heavy courses at a time.

The features that matter for exam week are the ones that produce practice material: study guides, quizzes, flashcards, and Audio Overviews. I dumped a dense methods chapter plus two lecture decks into one notebook the night before a quiz. The Audio Overview gave me a 12-minute conversation I could listen to while walking. The generated quiz questions were grounded in the exact wording of the slides, which is what most professors test.

It does not do spaced repetition. It does not record live lectures. Those are real limits. But for the core problem — "I have a pile of course materials and three days left" — nothing free comes closer.

## Force recall with flashcards — Anki or Knowt

Summaries feel productive. They are not. The research on learning is clear: retrieval practice beats re-reading. That is why flashcards still win for closed-book exams.

Anki remains the long-term choice if you are willing to build the cards (or import them). It is free forever on desktop and Android. Knowt and Quizlet are faster when you want AI to generate the cards from your notes or a PDF. Knowt's free tier still gives unlimited notes and Learn mode; the AI generation limits sit on the paid Ultra plan. Quizlet Plus (around $36/year in many regions) unlocks the stronger Magic Notes and practice tests.

I tested the same lecture notes in both NotebookLM and Knowt. NotebookLM gave me a clean study guide. Knowt turned the key definitions into a queue of cards that resurfaced the next day. For any course that tests discrete facts — anatomy, reactions, case law, vocabulary — the flashcard step is non-negotiable.

## Get unstuck without being spoon-fed — ChatGPT Study Mode or Claude

When you are stuck on a concept, the worst thing you can do is ask for the full answer. ChatGPT's Study Mode (free for logged-in users as of 2026) and Claude's Learning Mode are built to coach instead of dump. The difference is real: the models ask you questions back, refuse to hand over the solution immediately, and force you to show your reasoning.

I used both on the same hard problem set. Study Mode kept me in the problem longer. That is the point. Use the free tiers first. Only pay for Plus or Pro if you are hitting daily limits during a heavy exam week.

## A practical exam-week stack that stays free for most people

You do not need eight tools. A stack that covers the three jobs looks like this:

1. **Capture and ground** — NotebookLM with your actual slides, readings, and cleaned lecture notes.
2. **Force recall** — Anki or Knowt for anything the exam will test by memory.
3. **Unstick a concept** — ChatGPT Study Mode or Claude when you hit a wall.

Otter.ai is worth adding only if you have lectures you cannot afford to miss and the free minutes are still available. Everything else is optional until one of these three stops solving the problem in front of you.

## What still does not work

AI will happily summarize a chapter you never read. The summary is useless if you cannot explain it yourself. The tools remove the friction of turning raw material into reviewable form. They do not remove the need to think. Privacy is another constraint: everything you upload sits on someone else's servers. For most undergrad work that is fine. For anything sensitive, keep it local.
